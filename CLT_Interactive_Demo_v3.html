Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

When independent random samples of size n are repeatedly drawn from a population with a finite mean μ and finite variance σ², the sampling distribution of the sample mean becomes approximately Gaussian as n becomes large — even when the population itself is not Gaussian.

If the population is Gaussian, the sampling distribution of the sample mean is Gaussian for every sample size.
What the CLT is saying:
The population does not have to be Gaussian. What becomes approximately Gaussian is the sampling distribution of the sample mean as the sample size increases.

Notice the distinction: “What does the population look like?” versus “What does the distribution of x̄ look like after repeated sampling?”

Try this

  1. Choose the Normal population. Compare n = 5 and n = 30. Does the Gaussian overlay fit at both sample sizes?
  2. Choose the Right-skewed population. Start with n = 5, then try 10, 30 and 50. What happens to the shape of the sampling distribution?
  3. Try the Bimodal population. Compare a small and a large n. Does the sampling distribution retain the population's bimodality?
  4. For one population, keep n fixed and increase the number of repeated samples. What changes: the underlying sampling distribution, or how clearly you see it?
  5. Compare the observed SD of x̄ with σ/√n. How close are they?
